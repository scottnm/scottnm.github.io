Selected Excerpts: Sabbath as Resistance

[ 2026-10-24 ]

I read "Sabbath as Resistance: Saying No to the Culture of Now" by Walter Brueggemann.

Here's a collection of things I highlighted or tagged in my first readthrough of the book.

⚠️️ ** DISCLAIMER ** ⚠️

This book is a work of Christian and Jewish theological theory: what the sabbath commandment meant in its historical context and what, to the author, it means today.

I am neither Christian nor Jewish, and that will of course color the way I felt reading this book, and what I felt was noteworthy. I intend for the below notes to be read with a tone of good-faith, cooperation between author and reader.

And a special note on names:

  1. Brueggemann refers to his god by the name of YHWH throughout the book. This is a pretty common practice to avoid disrespecting the name of god. Another common form, not employed by this book, is just to omit letters: "G-d." In quotes, I will leave Brueggemann's chosen names in intact ("YHWH" and rarely "God") as a show of that good faith and respect. In my own notes, I'll stick with the classic lowercase "god" (again, I hope as a sign of respect that I'm not masquerading in false tradition or being inauthentic.)

  2. When the book refers to "Israel" it is referring to the ethnic tribe of Israelites and/or god's chosen people, and not the present (2026) political entity and nation-state.

Excerpts

Preface, p. xi, quoting Michael Fishbane, Sacred Attunement: A Jewish Theology (Chicago: University of Chicago Press, 2008)

Defining the Sabbath as a sphere of inaction:

One enters the sphere of inaction through divestment, and this release affects all the elements of the workaday sphere. Business activity and exchange of money are forbidden, and one is urged not just to desist from commerce but to develop more interior spheres of settling the mind from this type of agitation[...] Slowly, under these multiple conditions, a sense of inaction takes over, and the day does not merely mark the stoppage of work or celebrate the completion of creation, but enforces the value that the earth is a gift of divine creativity, given to humankind in sacred trust. On the Sabbath, the practical benefits of technology are laid aside, and one tries to stand in the cycle of natural time, without manipulation or interference. To the degree possible, one must attempt to brin the qualities of inaction and rest into the heart and mind. [...]The Sabbath is thus a period of sacred stasis, a duration of sanctity through the cultivation of inaction in body and spirit. [...] The heart beat of repose may thus suffuse the mind and limbs of one's being, and generate an inner balance poised on quietude and a settled spirit.

I've included this full quote from Fishbane because I personally never had a firm, purposeful definition of the Sabbath beyond showing deference to god. I think it's useful and connects this book back to Oliver Burkeman's "Four Thousand Weeks" which is the reason I started reading it in the first place.

To highlight an excerpt of an excerpt, I think this is the part I find most "catching":

On the Sabbath, the practical benefits of technology are laid aside, and one tries to stand in the cycle of natural time, without manipulation or interference.

Standing in the cycle of natural time sounds quite special.

Preface, p. xiii

Israel cannot store up bread for more than a day; except (big "except"!) on the sixth day Israel may store up enough for the seventh day so that it can rest on that day (vv. 22-24). This unexpected provision is surely a sign that this bread for life is not under the demanding governance of the Pharaoh; it is under the sustaining rule of the creator God. Even in the wilderness with scarce resources, God mandates a pause for Sabbath for the community [...] The conclusion affirmed by the narrative is that wherever YHWH governs as an alternative to Pharaoh, there the restfulness of YHWH effectively counters the restless anxiety of Pharaoh.

A key historical perspective the book wants to share is that the Pharaoh was effectively an anxious capitalist. He accrued wealth and resources not to purpose, but for the intrinsic "line goes up" value that we're quite familiar with among the ultra wealthy in modern day capitalist economies.

I found it interesting that this book frames god's rules around disallowing resource stockpiling as less of an exercise in showing respect to god but in showing trust in god. It could be argued that this is a distinction without a difference, but it felt significant to me. Relinquishing control to god not (just) because god is a jealous god constantly asking you to prove yourself with arbitrary loyalty tests, but because the act of relinquishing this control ultimately gives god's people peace.

Families, largely contained in market ideology, think of themselves as helpless before the requirements of such commitment. In context, it requires [...] enormous communal resolve to resist the demand.

Ch. 3: Resistance to Coercion, p. 38

Moses understands, as do the prophets after him, that being in the land poses for Israel a conflict between two economic systems, each of which views the land differently. On the one hand, the land is regarded as property and possession to be bought and sold and traded and used. On the other hand, in a context of covenant, the land is a birthright and an inheritance, one's own land as a subset of the larger inheritance of the whole people of God. If the land is possession, then the proper way of life is to acquire more. If the land is inheritance, then the proper way of life is to enhance the neighborhood and the extended family so that all members may enjoy the good produce of the land. It is clear which of these perspectives was appropriate to Sinai. But in its amnesia, Israel may forget its covenantal frame of reference and generate an economy that is anti-neighborly in order to have more and more.

I think I highlighted this passage because I felt like it expressed a nice sentiment, but in an unconvincing way.

Really, it's just restating the book's premise that Sinai was in some way rooted in establishing a moral code that prioritized rest and social support systems over commodity economies... BUT I felt like the metaphor was kind of shaky.

How is inheritance and birthright different from possession? I think most people would claim that inheritance and birthright are just one of many justifications which root an argument of possession: "I own this car because it was passed to me from higher authority/lineage/whatever" vs "I own this car because I purchased it with money from my part-time job at Old Navy."

I'm not saying it's an argument that CAN'T be made, but I don't think the Brueggemann did it (NOTE: I'm returning to these notes after having read the full thing but without deep diving into all context surrounding the notes. It's possible, I missed where this metaphor was better supported elsewhere, but I don't recall it and don't see it closely collocated with this passage.)

I think the argument the book makes later—the importance of the commandment "Thou shalt not Covet"—is a way more compelling argument to the tension between possession and the abstractions of possession to pointless expansion. And of course, the idea of inherited possession feels like it comes into conflict with Christian theological teachings about coveting worldly possessions. Despite being an Old Testament scholar, Brueggemann is a Christian, so I'd be interested to see how those two points of tension are held in his mind. I'm not familiar with what a more orthodox Jewish scholar might say about the effect of possession on spiritual practice.

Ch. 3: Resistance to Coercion, p. 40

As in Exodus 20, all are to rest [...]. But a phrase is added, "that they may rest like you." Sabbath is a great day of equality when all are equally at rest. Not all are equal in production. Some perform much more effectively than others. Not all are equal in consumption. Some have greater access to consumer goods. In a society defined by production and consumption, there are huge gradations of performance and, therefore, of worth and significance. In such a social system everyone is coerced to perform better—product more, consume more—be a good shopper! Such valuing, of course creates "haves" and "have-nots," significant and insignificant, rich and poor, people with access and people denied access.

Ch. 3: Resistance to Coercion, p. 44

First in Deuteronomy 15:1-18, Moses enunciates the most radical extrapolation of Sabbath in the entire Bible. Every seven years, in an enactment of the "sabbatic principle," Israel is enjoyed to cancel debts on poor people. The intention in this radical act of "seven" is that there should be no permanent underclass in Israel (v. 4). Moses, in this instruction, anticipates resistance to the radical extrapolation of Sabbath, that Israelites may be "hard hearted" and "tight fisted" (v. 7). But that is because they have fallen into coercive patters whereby the poor are targeted as objects of economic abuse rather than seen as Sabbath neighbors. Moses counters such resistance, however by appeal to the exodus memory (v. 15). On the basis of that Sabbath memory, Israel is invited to "give liberally" (v. 10) and "provide liberally" (v. 14).

Ch. 5: Resistance to Multitasking, p. 58

When I was growing up in Rural Saline County, Missouri, "Mr. G", our town grocer, and his wife always sat up front in church. Every Sunday, during the last five minutes of the sermon by the pastor (my father), Mr. G. and his wife would rather ceremoniously walk the long aisle to the back of the church and leave. They did not mind the distraction of their maneuver to everyone else at worship. The reason they left is that the other church in town, the Missouri Synod Lutheran Church, got out of service thirty minutes earlier than we "E & R" Christians finished worship. As a kid, I often wondered how often Mr. G had looked at his watch during the service to be sure he left on time to receive Lutheran trade and Lutheran money.

There's a lot of charitable and uncharitable ways to read this anecdote. But I think the most important thing that stood out to me reading this was that this passage is denigrating what's expressed as a rebuke of the Sabbath (prioritizing commerce over worship), but there's that little inkling of personal distaste that Brueggemann couldn't leave out. It wasn't just that they prioritized their grocery store Sunday hours over the final 5 minutes of church, but that their ritual involved conspicuously leaving and distracting others. It feels like a weird detail of the story that kind of bleeds the message between "Mr. G is annoying and therefore you should not like him" and "Mr. G is violating Sabbath and you should learn a lesson from this."

Ch. 5: Resistance to Multitasking, p. 58

In my horizon, the most unwelcome form of multitasking is with the cell phone—on the phone while at dinner with a guest or while driving. But a much more poisonous form of multitasking is taking notes during a church service... not notes on the sermon but a grocery list or calls to return or deals to make. [...]Such practice (multitasking) yields a divided self, with full attention given to nothing.

I remember highlighting this because I generally vibed with this but felt the "grocery list" call out to not be on equal footing with the rest of the multitasking poison. I think it just feels like morally compromised to be planning to feed your family than it does to be planning business. If I have to concede to consistency within the book's perspective, its still not something to do on the Sabbath and that grocery list would be better done as part of the extra preparations you do on the day preceding the Sabbath. Still felt like a weird neg to moms in church, but I suppose that's my personal quibble.

Ch. 5: Resistance to Multitasking, p. 59

To serve God and wealth at the same time is impossible. It is like keeping Sabbath and at the same time planning for commerce. It is like making deep love but all the while watching the clock. It is like praising Jesus while preying on the poor. Such multitasking with a divided heart means that there is no real work stoppage, no interruption in the frantic attempt to get ahead.

Ch. 6: Sabbath and the Tenth Commandment, p. 73

Though the term "covet" is not used in [the story of seizure of Naboth's property by King Ahab and Queen Jezebel in 1 Kings], it well exposits the commandment. [...] The royal couple, completely impervious to such claims, will stop at nothing until they are able to "take possession" of [Naboth's] ancestral heritage. This narrative makes it clear that "coveting" is not only a desire for, but the active capacity to seize, what one desires.

I highlighted this passage because I felt like it set out to clarify what "coveting" means and then never really justified that clearer definition.

It states it as a matter of fact that this story is about coveting, then doesn't really make a point to connect it back to any other definition to support that, and uses the story as a concrete example of coveting with which to further clarify the definition.

I don't know. It just bugged me. I think this is the kind of thing you see a lot in non-fiction that isn't often critically thought about because its presented through the format of a book. And I'm particularly gullible to ideas presented in books, so I guess I was extra annoyed by it.

Ch. 6: Sabbath and the Tenth Commandment, p. 74

Micah is a spokesperson for the small agrarian peasants who engage, as they are able, in resistance against the urban elites in Jerusalem.

I remember feeling like I saw this language of "urban elites" come up a few times in this book. It wasn't clear to me if that was Brueggemann's attempt at providing the perspective of Micah and his story's central characters or Brueggemann's own characterization of how life was like in Micah's time.

I think the phrase "urban elites" (especially expressed in the context of Jews and Judaism) rubs me the wrong way because of its close proximity to antisemitic rhetoric.

I am definitely not suggesting Brueggemann is engaging with that rhetoric or intends to bolster it, but it was a part of the book that bugged me.

Ch. 6: Sabbath and the Tenth Commandment, p. 76

The vulnerable peasants [of Isaiah's poetry] are then removed from their land and denied a livelihood, and now coveters can bask in their newly secured isolated self-indulgence. The prophetic judgement pertains to such rural displacement; in our time, the same crisis might refer to urban gentrification that dislocates the poor and the vulnerable. The poetry traces the destruction, by acquisitiveness, of a viable neighborly infrastructure.

Ch. 6: Sabbath and the Tenth Commandment, p. 77

Thus in both Torah and wisdom tradition, the moving of such boundary markers was prohibited, as subset of the tenth commandment.

I remember highlighting this because I found it funny to think of god dishing out parcels of land like the city and weighing over property line disputes.

"Hey! What do you think you're doing? Your Snoopy Christmas decoration is hanging over the property line. Move it or I'll move my foot so far up your ass..."

Think of that but between agrarian Israelites. Heh.

Ch. 6: Sabbath and the Tenth Commandment, p. 81

In Ephesians 5:3-5, greed is twice listed among the distractions to be avoided: But fornication and impurity of any kind, or greed, must not even be mentioned among you , as is proper among saints. Entirely out of place is obscene, silly, and vulgar talk, but instead let there be thanksgiving. Be sure of this, that no fornicator, or impure person, or anyone who is greedy (that is, an idolater) has any inheritance in the kingdom of Christ and of God. (5:3-5)

Another spot where I felt like nitpicking: yes greed is mentioned twice, but so is fornication and impurity. Brueggemann goes on to reflect on the significance of greed to his reading but ignores the collocated concerns of fornication and impurity.

From my perspective, the more interesting question I came away with was why are these three things paired together in Ephesians. What is the relationship between greed and fornication? What is the relationship between general impurity and these two? Why the sudden mention of obscenity, silliness, and vulgarity. It feels like a motley crew of general moral concerns rather than a collection of things implicitly bound together.

I could probably answer my own question by just reading Ephesians but I don't wanna... woe, is me.

Ch. 6: Sabbath and the Tenth Commandment, p. 81

Almost incidentally and without calling attention to it, these testimonies identify idolatry that is rejected in commandment one and greed that is rejected in commandment 10. The two, ideology and greed, are equated because both of them reduce livable reality to commodity. Idolatry is [...]

This one I highlighted just because I felt like being a nitpicky rascal.

  1. I think in the second sentence, Brueggemann typo'd "idolatry" as "ideology". Oops!
  2. I found it funny that he mixed "commandment one" and "commandment 10" in the same sentence (note the difference in full-word "one" vs Arabic numeral "10").

I don't really care or cite this as evidence of any lack of quality from the author or editorial team. I was tickled that it might suggest a scrappier editorial team than a major publishing house might have.

I also felt like by this point in the book, I was starting to grow a little weary of circling some of the same points. It felt like in making these mistakes, Brueggemann was probably undergoing his own weariness nearing the end of his initial draft. Different kinds of weariness but a fun connection.

Ch. 6: Sabbath and the Tenth Commandment, p. 87

Sabbath is a big no [...] to the worship of commodity; it is [a big] no to the pursuit of commodity. But it is more than no. Sabbath is the regular, disciplined, visible, concrete yes to the neighborly reality of the community beloved by God. We used to sing the hymn "Take Time to Be Holy." But perhaps we should be singing "Take time to be human." Or finally, "Take time." Sabbath is taking time... time to be holy... time to be human.

The idea of "taking time to be human" and "taking time" represented as religious values really struck a chord with me. It feels rare that religious (even humanist religions) would celebrate the "human" experience and the value in celebrating that (even insofar as its an act of celebrating and revering god's creation.) I found this passage to be really beautiful.
