Selected Excerpts: Four Thousand Weeks by Oliver Burkeman

[ 2026-10-09 ]

I read "Four Thousand Weeks" by Oliver Burkeman.

I have to return the book to the library soon, so this page collects the excerpts I highlighted (and sometimes annotated!)

Summary

All in all, my takeaway from reading the book was...

  • You must come to terms with your own finitude. You're not guaranteed any time on earth and the limited time you have is wasted trying to feel productive.
  • Busyness is not equivalent to "productivity."
  • "Productivity" is not a measure of a life well-lived.
  • A lot of productivity advice is a sisyphean gesture at "getting more done" with the false promise that with enough effort you can eventually just be done.
  • "Getting more done" only results in having more to do.
  • It's wasted effort trying to get more done with the hope that one day you'll complete everything and finally feel a sense of calm.
  • Coming to grips that you'll never be "done" and finding effective ways to do less and not feeling bad about doing less is a more useful path to generally feeling ok.
  • The goal of "feeling ok" is not to feel a sense of constant calm and perfect control. Accept you will often feel anxious, feel overwhelmed, and fail. That's life baby.
  • Slow down, be patient, and avoid measuring success by how rarely you run into friction.
  • Multitasking is a scam. Serialize and focus where possible.

To me this book read as an "anti self-help book" and I quite liked it.

There was no homework, no system to reorient your life around, no "silver bullet" that Burkeman prescribes for how to fix yourself. Its a plea to a shared experience that people like myself who are constantly trying to feel organized may need to re-evaluate their goals around more practical and unavoidable (read: death) circumstances.

Excerpts

"In the Long Run, We're All Dead", pp. 13-14

Nobody in the history of humanity has ever achieved "work-life balance," whatever that might be, and you certainly won't get there by copying the "six things successful people do before 7:00am." The day will never arrive when you finally have everything under control—when the flood of emails has been contained; when your to-do lists have stopped getting longer; when you're meeting all your obligations at work and in your home life; when nobody's angry with you for missing a deadline or dropping the ball; and when the fully optimized person you've become can turn, at long last, to the things life is really supposed to be about. Let's start by admitting defeat

"The Limit-Embracing Life", pp. 18-19

Speaking on a supposed life of medieval peasants...

And though death was a constant presence, with lives cut short far more frequently than they are today, time wouldn't have felt in limited supply. You wouldn't have felt any pressure to find ways to "save" it. Nor would you have felt guilty for wasting it: if you took an afternoon break from threshing grain to watch a cockfight on the village green, it wouldn't have felt like you were shirking during "work time." And none of this was simply because things moved more slowly back then, or because medieval peasants were more relaxed or more resigned to their fate. It was because, so far as we can tell, they generally didn't experience time as an abstract entity—as a thing—at all.

I don't think Burkeman really made a persuasive case that medieval peasants really felt this way. And maybe that's where it failed for me, that it tried to jump to making guesses about the inner feelings of people long dead and that these guesses don't seem to have been backed up by any historical accounts, journals, etc.

Though to call attention to my own limitation here, there are notes and sources in the back of the book that maybe provide more context for this:

  • "an independent world of mathematically measurable sequences": Lewis Mumfor, Technics and Civilization (Chicago: University of Chicago Press, 2010), 15.
  • "Medieval people might speak of a task lasting a "Misere whyle": E. P. Thompson, "Time, Work-Discipline, and Industrial Capitalism," Past and Present 38 (1967): 81.

I do think the point I read out of it—that modern life organizes itself around an abstract concept of measurable and estimatable time that is kind of an illusion which affects our sense of reality—was interesting and resonated.

This was another passage I highlighted that felt boldly stated but kind of unsubstantiated. I get this isn't a "science" book, but I remember feeling like cool ideas expressed entirely with a "you just know, you know" attitude were off putting to me.

"The Efficiency Trap", pp. 51-52

In Silicon Valley [you] identify a "pain point"—one of the small annoyances resulting from (more jargon) the "friction" of daily life—and then offer a way to circumvent it. Thus Uber eliminates the "pain" of having to track down a number for your local taxi company [...]; digital wallet apps like Apple Pay remove the "pain" of having to reach into your bag for your physical wallet or cash. [...] you only need to commune with the screen.

I wouldn't be me if I didn't quibble with some hill not worth dying over.

The point Burkeman makes about over-valuing "convenience" and seeing every inconvenience as a problem to solve or something to fret over is good.

But damnit, I think the Apple Wallet one is a bad example. I feel like you could replace "Apple Wallet" with "credit card" and "physical wallet" with "cash" and it would sound like the same argument and seem just as wrong to me. I can't put my finger on it, but there's something more to convenience at play there. It's a freedom to go places and do things without having to panic because I've forgotten one crucial item (my wallet!) at home.

Why am I here white knighting for smartphone features? I don't know...

The rest of the section is good!

"Facing Finitude", p. 57

You can't delve far into the question of what it means to be a finite human [...] before encountering the philosopher who was more obsessed with the subject than any other think: Martin Heidegger. This is unfortunate [because] for more than a decade, starting in 1933, he was a card-carrying member of the Nazi Party. (The question of what this means for his philosophy is a fraught and fascinating one, but it would get us off track here.)

This is exactly the kind of tangent I would have loved to see in this book! Somewhere!! Even a summarized footnote!!!

While it might not make for a very focused and easy to follow sequence of introduction → argument → supporting evidence → argument → supporting evidence → conclusion, I think being intellectually curious about how philosophy manifests in the people who proselytize it, is hugely valuable in honestly and accurately painting a full picture of that philosophy.

Was his Nazism at odds with embracing human finitude? Is it in conflict to be a Nazi who will only do so much for his party, ideology, and myth-making? Or did the ideas bolster themselves? Does embracing human finitude lead to such a strict prioritization on what it means to be with limited time that you might feel justified in total "death of self" in favor of fascistically serving your ideology?

I think that's a useful thought exercise for someone reading this book.

"Facing Finitude", p. 69

it's precisely the fact that getting married forecloses the possibility of meeting someone else [...] that makes marriage meaningful. The exhilaration that sometimes arises when you grasp this truth about finitude has been called the "joy of missing out."

"Becoming a Better Procrastinator", p. 76

Once you've selected [three tasks to focus on], all other incoming demands on your time must wait until one of the three items has been completed, thereby freeing up a slot. (It's also permissible to free up a slot by abandoning a project altogether if it isn't working out.) [Paraphrased from Benson and Barry's "Personal Kanban"]

This is one of the few times in the book where Burkeman shares a concrete system used by someone else for helping focus your efforts, and I thought it sounded like a good idea.

I suspect it'll help reduce multitasking for me. While there are still multiple things that could be done in parallel, I find most of my failed multitasking attempts are me procrasting with random interruptions and not me trying to do a number of pre-selected things in parallel. So, by deferring "new" concerns out of my awareness entirely until I've freed up a slot I think that will help with multitasking procrastination.

"Becoming a Better Procrastinator", pp. 79-80

procrastination is a strategy of emotional avoidance [...] The limitations we're trying to avoid when we engage in this self-defeating sort of procrastination frequently don't have anything to do with how much we'll be able to get done in the time available; usually, it's a matter of worrying that we won't have the talent to produce work of sufficient quality [...] or that in some other way things won't turn out as we want. [...] you can relax—because judged by the flawless standards of your imagination, you definitely won't do a good enough job. So you might as well make a start.

I'm a bad procrastinator and this section hit me pretty hard.

The procrastination that's hardest for me to avoid is the learned procrastination.

It's trying to write a new software feature or bug fix or design proposal knowing in the back of my mind that every other previous time I've done this, it's been an uphill battle of working through feedback and constructive criticism and problems I don't know how to solve. And of course none of those are real walls. They are constructive parts of the process. Things I should embrace and accept and cherish. But I've just built a terrible mental pattern of fear when I think about confronting that process.

Sometimes I wish I could give myself time-limited selective amnesia. But I don't think that's the point this book is trying to get across.

"The Intimate Interrupter", pp. 105-106

When you try to focus on something you deem important, you're forced to face your limits [...] you're obliged to give up your godlike fantasies and to experience your lack of power over things you care about. Perhaps, the cherished project will prove beyond your talents [...] and even if everything proceeds wonderfully, you couldn't have known in advance that it was going to do so, so you'll still have had to give up the feeling of being the master of your time [...] you've had had to allow yourself to risk feeling "claustrophobic, imprisoned, powerless, and constrained by reality."

Another resonant section that feels like it so strongly pulls into focus why its hard to commit to things. Fear is the mindkiller and its also unavoidable.

"The Intimate Interrupter", pp. 107-108

[being distracted] often doesn't feel especially fun, these days. But it doesn't need to feel fun. In order to dull the pain of finitude, it just needs to make you feel unconstrained. [... What we think of as distractions, social media, daydreaming, extra naps, busywork,] aren't the ultimate cause of our being distracted. They're just the places we go to seek relief from the discomfort of confronting limitation. [...] I wish I could reveal, at this point, the secret for uprooting the urge toward distraction—the way to have it not feel unpleasant to decide to hold your attention, for a sustained time, on something you value, or that you can't easily choose not to do. But the truth is I don't think there is one. The most effective way to sap distraction of its power is just to stop expecting things to be otherwise—to accept that this unpleasantness is simply what it feels like for finite humans to commit ourselves to the kinds of demanding and valuable tasks that force us to ocnfront our limited control over how our lives unfold.

Another killer couple of pages. This tone of acceptance is a lot of what makes this book so appealing to me. It's not suggesting you tools or hacks or strats to trick yourself into thinking this isn't hard. It's just trying to convince you that being hard isn't all that bad and you can deal with it.

Appendix: Compressed

These ten techniques are provided by the book's appendix. Rather than quote pretty much the entire thing, I'll just paraphrase it as a shortened reference for myself.

  1. Adopt a "fixed volume" approach to productivity.
    • keep limits on your todolist. if you need to space for limitless task tracking keep that in a separate list.
    • keep predetermined time boundaries for your daily work
  2. Serialize, Serialize, Serialize
    • focus on one big project at a time. Avoid bouncing around.
    • Be patient and sit with the anxiety of having to take something to completion.
  3. Decide in advance what to fail at
    • determine what things you don't care to excel at: kitchen tidiness, being the best world traveler, etc
  4. Focus on what you've already completed, not just on what's left to complete
    • keep a "done" list and collect that progress as a reminder that you're living your life.
  5. Consolidate your caring
    • consciously pick your battles in charity, activism, and politics
    • after picking your battles, feel comfortable not paying active attention to others.
  6. Embrace boring and single-purpose technology
    • delete social media apps
    • switch your smartphone screen to grayscale
    • choose devices with a single purpose that discourage aimless switching
  7. Seek out novelty in the mundane
    • pay more attention to every moment, however mundane
  8. Be a "researcher" in relationships
    • Try to understand the people you're with
    • Spend less time trying to use them to achieve your desired outcomes.
  9. Cultivate instantaneous generosity
    • whenever generous impulses arrive act on them immediately: give me, send words of praise, tell someone you love them.
  10. Practice doing nothing
    • set a timer for 5-10 minutes and then stop trying to do anything for that time.
